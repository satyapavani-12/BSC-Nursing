## In simple words

Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) are infections patients get while receiving care in a healthcare facility — they were not present or incubating at admission. They are also called nosocomial infections.

HAIs increase hospital stay, cost and risk of death. Nurses are frontline in preventing them through hand hygiene, asepsis and standard precautions.

## Common types of HAIs

| Type | Common sites | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| CAUTI | Urinary tract | Catheters |
| CLABSI | Bloodstream | Central lines |
| VAP | Lungs | Ventilators |
| SSI | Surgical wound | Surgery |
| Pneumonia | Lungs | Bedrest, aspiration |

## Risk factors

- Invasive devices (catheters, ventilators, central lines).
- Surgery and wounds.
- Weak immunity (elderly, babies, diabetes, cancer, steroids).
- Long hospital stay.
- Poor hand hygiene and asepsis.

## Prevention

- Hand hygiene before and after every patient contact.
- Aseptic technique for all invasive procedures.
- Remove catheters and lines as soon as no longer needed.
- Clean and disinfect equipment between patients.
- Standard precautions for every patient.

## Where the nurse uses this

- **Hand hygiene** — the single most important HAI prevention measure.
- **Catheter care** — assess daily if still needed; remove as soon as possible.
- **Ventilator care** — head of bed up, oral care, suction asepsis.
- **Wound care** — aseptic dressing changes; watch for redness, pus, fever.
- **Surveillance** — report infection signs (fever, redness, purulent discharge) early.

## 60-second revision

- HAI = infection acquired during healthcare, not at admission.
- Types: CAUTI, CLABSI, VAP, SSI, pneumonia.
- Risk: catheters, lines, surgery, weak immunity, long stay.
- Prevent: hand hygiene, asepsis, remove devices early.
- Report infection signs early.
