## In simple words

A review of literature is a careful study of what is already known about the problem. It prevents repetition, builds on prior work and helps to design the study.

## Purposes

- To know what is already known
- To find the gaps and avoid repeating the study
- To find a suitable tool and method
- To place the study in context and justify it
- To build a theoretical framework

## Sources

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Primary sources | Research articles, theses, dissertations, conference papers |
| Secondary sources | Textbooks, encyclopedias, reviews, indexes |
| Internet | PubMed, CINAHL, Google Scholar, IRINS, national portals |
| Institutional | INC and university libraries, WHO, ICMR journals |

## Database searching

- Define the **problem words, synonyms and keywords**
- Use Boolean operators: **AND** to narrow, **OR** to widen, **NOT** to exclude
- Apply limits: years, language, study type, population
- Retrieve the full text where possible

## Compiling a literature review

- Note author, year, place, sample, method, findings and limitations
- Group studies by method or theme
- Compare findings and note agreements and contradictions
- Identify gaps and link them to the current study
- Reference in an accepted style (APA or Vancouver) and avoid plagiarism

<Callout type="must" title="A review must show a gap, not a summary">
  The value of a review is a **critical, reasoned statement of what is unknown** and how the current study will answer it. Simply listing studies is not a review.
</Callout>

## 60-second revision

- Review finds known, unknown and gaps.
- Primary versus secondary sources.
- Boolean operators and database limits.
- Record and compile into themes.
- Write critically; cite correctly; end at the gap.
