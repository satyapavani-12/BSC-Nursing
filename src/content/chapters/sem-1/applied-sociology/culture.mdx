## In simple words

Culture is the shared beliefs, values, customs, language and way of life of a group of people. It is learned and passed from one generation to the next.

Culture affects what people eat, how they see illness, what treatment they accept, how they express pain and who makes decisions in the family.

Cultural competence means respecting and adapting care to each patient's culture.

## Components of culture

| Component | Meaning | Health example |
|---|---|---|
| Beliefs | Ideas accepted as true | Belief that illness is punishment |
| Values | Ideas of right and wrong | Value of family decision-making |
| Customs | Traditional practices | Diet during illness or pregnancy |
| Language | Way of communicating | Patient may not speak the nurse's language |
| Symbols | Objects with meaning | Religious symbols worn by patients |

## Ethnocentrism and cultural relativism

- **Ethnocentrism** — judging another culture by your own standards ("my way is right").
- **Cultural relativism** — understanding a culture on its own terms.

Nurses must avoid ethnocentrism and respect the patient's cultural beliefs, even when different from their own.

## Culture and health

- Food beliefs affect diet and recovery.
- Some cultures prefer home remedies before hospital care.
- Gender norms may affect who can examine the patient.
- Pain expression varies — some cultures show pain openly, others hide it.

</Callout>

## Where the nurse uses this

- **Ask about beliefs** — gently ask what the patient thinks caused the illness and what they expect from treatment.
- **Respect food customs** — arrange suitable meals; fasting may matter for religious reasons.
- **Respect gender norms** — offer a same-gender caregiver when possible for intimate care.
- **Use interpreters** — if language differs, use a trained interpreter; do not rely on children.
- **Include the family** — many cultures make health decisions as a family.
