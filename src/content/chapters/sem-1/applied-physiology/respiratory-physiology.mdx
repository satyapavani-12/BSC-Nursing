## In simple words

The lungs are two soft bags hanging in a sealed box. The box is the chest, and its floor is the **diaphragm**. When the diaphragm contracts and flattens, the box gets bigger, pressure inside falls below atmospheric pressure, and air rushes in. When the diaphragm relaxes, the box shrinks and air flows out. That is all quiet breathing is.

Inside the lungs, air reaches about 300 million tiny sacs called **alveoli**. Each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries, and the wall between air and blood is less than one micrometre thick. Oxygen crosses into the blood, carbon dioxide crosses out. The total alveolar surface is roughly **70 square metres** — about half a tennis court folded into your chest.

Physiology matters clinically because the lungs do two jobs at once: they bring oxygen in and they control the body’s acid level by blowing off carbon dioxide. A patient who breathes too slowly retains CO₂ and turns acidotic; a patient who breathes too fast washes CO₂ out and turns alkalotic.

## Mechanics of breathing

**Inspiration is active.** The diaphragm contributes about 75% of the work; the external intercostals lift the ribs. Accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes) are recruited only in distress — their use is a warning sign.

**Expiration is passive at rest**, driven by elastic recoil. In asthma or COPD, expiration becomes active and prolonged because the airways narrow.

Two forces keep the lungs stuck to the chest wall: **pleural fluid** and **negative intrapleural pressure** (about −5 cm H₂O at rest). Puncture the pleura and air enters — a **pneumothorax** — and the lung collapses.

**Compliance** is how easily the lung stretches. **Surfactant**, made by type II alveolar cells, lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse. Premature babies lack surfactant and develop respiratory distress syndrome.

## Lung volumes and capacities

| Measurement | Normal adult value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (TV) | about 500 mL | Air moved in one quiet breath |
| Inspiratory reserve volume | about 3,000 mL | Extra air after a normal breath in |
| Expiratory reserve volume | about 1,100 mL | Extra air forced out after a normal breath out |
| Residual volume (RV) | about 1,200 mL | Air that never leaves the lung |
| Vital capacity (VC) | about 4,600 mL | Maximum breath out after maximum breath in |
| Functional residual capacity | about 2,300 mL | ERV plus RV — air left after quiet expiration |
| Total lung capacity | about 5,800 mL | Everything the lung can hold |
| Minute ventilation | about 6 L/min | TV × respiratory rate (500 × 12) |

Dead space is about **150 mL** — air in the conducting airways that never reaches an alveolus. Alveolar ventilation is therefore (500 − 150) × 12, about **4.2 L/min**.

## Exchange of gases and transport in blood

Diffusion across the alveolar membrane depends on surface area, thickness, and the pressure gradient. Oxygen partial pressure is **100 mmHg** in alveoli and **40 mmHg** in venous blood; carbon dioxide is **45 mmHg** in blood and **40 mmHg** in alveoli.

Oxygen is carried in two ways: about **1.5% dissolved** in plasma and about **98.5% bound to haemoglobin**. Each gram of haemoglobin carries **1.34 mL of oxygen**; with 15 g/dL the blood holds roughly **20 mL O₂ per 100 mL**.

Carbon dioxide travels as **bicarbonate (70%)**, **carbamino compounds (23%)** and **dissolved (7%)**. Carbonic anhydrase in red cells makes the bicarbonate route fast.

The curve shifts **right** (more unloading in tissues) with high CO₂, acidosis, raised temperature and high 2,3-DPG — the Bohr effect. It shifts **left** with cold, alkalosis and fetal haemoglobin.

## Control of breathing

The **medulla oblongata** holds the primary respiratory centre, assisted by the **pons** (pneumotaxic centre). Normal automatic rhythm comes from here.

Central chemoreceptors respond to **rising CO₂ and falling pH in cerebrospinal fluid** — this is the main drive to breathe in health. Peripheral chemoreceptors in the **carotid bodies** and aortic bodies respond to a fall in oxygen below about 60 mmHg. In chronic COPD the CO₂ drive is blunted, and the patient relies on the hypoxic drive; giving uncontrolled high-flow oxygen can therefore slow breathing.

<Callout type="must" title="Key concept">
  CO₂, not oxygen, is the main chemical stimulus to breathe in a healthy person. Oxygen only becomes a strong stimulus when it falls below 60 mmHg.
</Callout>

## Common disturbances

- **Hypoxia** — low oxygen in tissues: cyanosis, confusion, tachycardia, then bradycardia.
- **Type 1 respiratory failure** — PaO₂ below 60 mmHg with normal or low PaCO₂ (pneumonia, pulmonary embolism).
- **Type 2 respiratory failure** — PaO₂ below 60 with PaCO₂ above 50 mmHg (COPD, opioid overdose).
- **Acidosis** — hypoventilation retains CO₂ and lowers pH.
- **Alkalosis** — hyperventilation washes out CO₂ and raises pH.

## Where the nurse uses this

- **Oxygen therapy:** titrate to target saturation (94–98%, or 88–92% in COPD) and never remove the hypoxic drive without monitoring.
- **Pulse oximetry:** SpO₂ below 90% is significant; cold fingers and nail polish give false low readings.
- **Positioning:** upright Fowler’s position lowers the diaphragm and improves tidal volume.
- **Incentive spirometry and deep breathing:** prevent atelectasis after abdominal surgery.
- **Suctioning and chest physiotherapy:** clear secretions and improve alveolar ventilation.
- **Early warning scores:** rising respiratory rate is often the first sign of deterioration.

## 60-second revision

- TV 500 mL × 12/min = 6 L/min; dead space 150 mL.
- Alveolar ventilation = (500 − 150) × 12 ≈ 4.2 L/min.
- VC 4,600 mL; RV 1,200 mL; TLC 5,800 mL.
- O₂: 98.5% on Hb, 1.5% dissolved; 1.34 mL per gram of Hb.
- CO₂: 70% bicarbonate, 23% carbamino, 7% dissolved.
- Medulla drives breathing; CO₂ is the main stimulus.
- Right shift of the curve = more O₂ to tissues.
