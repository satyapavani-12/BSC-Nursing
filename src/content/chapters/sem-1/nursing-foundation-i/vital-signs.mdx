## In simple words

Vital signs are the body’s basic signals that life is working normally — or not. They are called “vital” because a change can be the first warning that a patient is getting worse. Nurses measure them on admission, before and after procedures, after medicines that affect the heart or breathing, and at fixed intervals.

The classic four are **temperature, pulse, respiration and blood pressure**. Modern practice adds **oxygen saturation (SpO₂)** and often **pain** as the fifth vital sign.

<Callout type="must" title="Most important table in this chapter">
  Learn these adult values by heart. A single abnormal value is a reason to inform the doctor — you do not wait for the next round.
</Callout>

## Normal adult values

| Vital sign | Normal adult range | Instrument |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature (oral) | 36.5–37.5 °C | Thermometer |
| Pulse | 60–100 beats/min | Fingers / monitor |
| Respiration | 12–20 breaths/min | Observation |
| Blood pressure | 90–120 / 60–80 mmHg | Sphygmomanometer |
| SpO₂ | 95–100% | Pulse oximeter |
| Pain | 0–10 scale (0 = none) | Pain scale |

## 1. Temperature

The **hypothalamus** is the body’s thermostat. Heat is produced by metabolism and lost through the skin, lungs and urine.

| Site | Normal range | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Oral | 36.5–37.5 °C | Wait 15–30 min after hot/cold drinks |
| Axillary | 36.0–37.0 °C | Safest, but slowest and least accurate |
| Rectal | 37.0–38.0 °C | Most accurate; avoid in diarrhoea or rectal surgery |
| Tympanic | about 37.0 °C | Quick; correct ear position matters |
| Temporal | about 37.0 °C | Non-invasive, used in children |

**Fever (pyrexia)** is a temperature above the normal range. Types:

- **Continuous** — remains high, varies by less than 1 °C (e.g. pneumonia).
- **Remittent** — varies by more than 1 °C but never becomes normal (e.g. typhoid).
- **Intermittent** — alternates between fever and normal (e.g. malaria).
- **Relapsing** — fever for days, then normal for days, then fever again.

Hypothermia is a core temperature below 35 °C.

## 2. Pulse

The pulse is the wave of blood felt when the heart contracts. It tells you the **rate, rhythm, volume, tension and equality** of the heartbeat.

Common sites:

| Site | Where to feel it | When it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Radial | Thumb side of the wrist | Routine adult pulse |
| Carotid | Side of the neck | Emergency, cardiac arrest |
| Apical | 5th intercostal space, left mid-clavicular line | Infants, irregular pulse, before digoxin |
| Brachial | Inner side of the elbow | Blood pressure |
| Femoral | Groin | Cardiac arrest, shock |
| Popliteal | Behind the knee | Circulation to the leg |
| Posterior tibial | Behind the medial ankle | Foot circulation |
| Dorsalis pedis | Top of the foot | Foot circulation |

<Callout type="imp" title="Counting rule">
  Count for a full **60 seconds** when the rhythm is irregular. For a regular pulse you may count for 30 seconds and double it.
</Callout>

Terms to know: **tachycardia** (above 100/min), **bradycardia** (below 60/min), **dysrhythmia** (irregular rhythm), **bounding pulse** (strong volume), **thready pulse** (weak, easily lost).

## 3. Respiration

Respiration is counted without telling the patient, because breathing changes when people know they are being watched. Observe the rise and fall of the chest for one minute.

Observe **rate, rhythm, depth and character**. Terms:

- **Tachypnoea** — fast breathing.
- **Bradypnoea** — slow breathing.
- **Apnoea** — no breathing.
- **Dyspnoea** — difficulty breathing.
- **Orthopnoea** — breathlessness when lying flat.
- **Cheyne–Stokes** — cycles of deep breathing then apnoea.

## 4. Blood pressure

Blood pressure is the force of blood against artery walls.

- **Systolic** — pressure when the heart contracts.
- **Diastolic** — pressure when the heart relaxes.
- **Pulse pressure** — systolic minus diastolic (normally 30–50 mmHg).

| Category | Systolic | Diastolic |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | 90–120 | 60–80 |
| Elevated | 120–129 | below 80 |
| Hypertension stage 1 | 130–139 | 80–89 |
| Hypertension stage 2 | 140 or more | 90 or more |
| Hypotension | below 90 | below 60 |

**Correct technique (very commonly asked):**

1. Rest the patient for 5 minutes; no caffeine or smoking for 30 minutes.
2. Support the arm at heart level, palm up.
3. Wrap the cuff so the bladder covers about 40% of the arm circumference.
4. Place the stethoscope over the brachial artery, not under the cuff.
5. Inflate 20–30 mmHg above the point where the pulse disappears.
6. Deflate slowly, 2–3 mmHg per second.
7. Record systolic at the first sound and diastolic at the last sound.
8. Take two readings and average them.

Common errors: cuff too small (false high), arm unsupported (false high), arm above heart level (false low), deflating too fast (inaccurate), patient talking (false high).

## 5. Oxygen saturation (SpO₂)

A pulse oximeter shines light through a finger to estimate how much oxygen is carried by haemoglobin. Normal is **95–100%**.

It can read falsely low in cold fingers, dark nail polish, poor circulation, or movement. It does **not** measure ventilation — a patient can have a normal SpO₂ and still be breathing out too much carbon dioxide.

## 60-second revision

- T 36.5–37.5 °C · P 60–100/min · R 12–20/min · BP 90–120/60–80 · SpO₂ 95–100%.
- Hypothalamus controls temperature.
- Carotid pulse in emergencies; apical before digoxin.
- Count pulse and respiration for a full minute if irregular.
- BP cuff: right size, arm at heart level, deflate 2–3 mmHg per second.
- Always record and report abnormal values.
